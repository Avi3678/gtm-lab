Finvest / Marketing

Treasury bills are boring. That is the marketing opportunity.

Avi Bedi / November 16, 2026 / Outside-in analysis

My read: Finvest sells the least exciting good decision in personal finance, so its marketing should make boring feel smart instead of trying to make it feel exciting.

What I noticed

Finvest, a Y Combinator company covered by TechCrunch, lets anyone buy US Treasury bills in minutes - founder and CEO Shivam Bharuka started it after watching banks pay pennies on savings while T-bill rates ran high. Its own jobs board lists a Marketing Intern in San Francisco. The product's challenge is category education: most young savers have never bought a T-bill and assume it is complicated or not for them.

I'd test a plain-arithmetic content series aimed at students and first-job savers: the actual dollars-and-cents difference between a savings account and a T-bill ladder on realistic amounts, told without jargon or hype, in the formats students already consume. The skepticism of a young audience is the point - content that respects it converts better than content that oversells. Measure signups per piece by channel. That is a messaging hypothesis, not a claim that Finvest's current marketing is jargon-heavy.

What I'd do in my first 30 days

Days 1-7. Open an account and buy a T-bill myself, note every confusing step, and study which channels currently drive signups.

Days 8-20. Produce the first three plain-arithmetic pieces for student audiences, distribute through campus and personal-finance communities, and track signup response.

Days 21-30. Compare performance by format and channel, refine the series, and hand the team a repeatable content calendar with the research behind it.

My portfolio research placed second nationally in the SIFMA Capitol Hill Challenge, so making fixed-income concepts legible to skeptical audiences is something I have practiced competitively. Marketing a product that genuinely helps young savers would be easy to stand behind.
